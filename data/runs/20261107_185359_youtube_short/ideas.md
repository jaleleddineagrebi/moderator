# Video ideas: Psychology & human behavior (biases, habits, social dynamics)

## 1. Why You Get Great Ideas in the Shower
Explains the 'shower effect' where mind-wandering during routine, non-demanding tasks releases dopamine and shifts the brain into the default mode network, unlocking creative epiphanies. Shows fast-paced b-roll of flowing water, city walks, and sudden realizations.

Hook (en): Stop trying so hard to think.
Hook (fr): Arrête de forcer tes réflexions.
Why now: Creativity and overthinking hacks regularly go viral on Shorts because everyone experiences sudden shower insights; recent 2022-2023 cognitive psychology experiments by Zac Irving confirmed the exact mechanics behind mind-wandering and task incubation.
Keywords: shower effect psychology, default mode network creativity, pourquoi bonnes idées sous la douche, psychologie créativité cerveau
- Irving et al. (2022) - 'The Shower Effect: Mind Wandering Facilitates Creative Incubation During Moderately Engaging Activities', Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts
- Christoff et al. (2016) - 'Mind-wandering as spontaneous thought', Nature Reviews Neuroscience

## 2. Why Big Plates Trick Your Appetite
Reveals the Delboeuf Illusion, showing how the exact same portion size looks drastically smaller on a larger plate, unconsciously forcing your brain to serve more food and feel hungrier. Visualized with geometric circle contrasts and aerial shots of plated meals.

Hook (en): Your plate is controlling your appetite.
Hook (fr): Ton assiette contrôle ton appétit.
Why now: Everyday health and perceptual illusions have exceptionally high shareability and comment rates due to people testing the visual illusion directly on screen in the first 5 seconds.
Keywords: delboeuf illusion food, plate size illusion psychology, illusion optique assiette faim, biais cognitif alimentation
- Delboeuf, F. J. (1865) - 'Note sur certaines illusions d'optique'
- Wansink & van Ittersum (2012) - 'Plate Size and Color Suggestibility: The Delboeuf Illusion's Bias on Serving and Eating Behaviour', Journal of Consumer Research

## 3. Why Strangers Like You More Than You Think
Breaks down the 'Liking Gap'—a systematic cognitive bias where people consistently underestimate how much a conversation partner enjoyed talking to them and how positive their first impression was. Uses urban candid pedestrian interactions and coffee shop footage.

Hook (en): They liked you more than you think.
Hook (fr): Ils t'ont apprécié bien plus que prévu.
Why now: Social anxiety and self-doubt content are evergreen drivers of high engagement and saves on Shorts; this provides instant emotional relief backed by solid empirical data from Boothby et al.
Keywords: the liking gap psychology, first impression bias, le liking gap psychologie, anxiété sociale première impression
- Boothby et al. (2018) - 'The Liking Gap in Conversations: Do People Like Us More Than We Think?', Psychological Science
- Reagan et al. (2021) - 'The Liking Gap Over Time', Journal of Experimental Social Psychology
